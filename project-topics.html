How to use this list

Each topic below states a managerial question that is currently debated in practice and comes with a starter paper from a leading marketing journal. The paper gives you the theory, a validated way to manipulate and measure the key constructs, and the expected direction of the effect; read at least its introduction, one key experiment, and the general discussion. You do not replicate the paper — you adapt the question to a product, a context, and a decision that a manager would face today.

Designing the study is your job. For every topic, your group decides what exactly to manipulate in the two conditions, which outcome to measure, and — the two course questions of 6  Mechanisms & Heterogeneity — for whom the effect should be larger (the moderator you choose and justify) and why it occurs (the mediator you choose and justify). The requirements that apply to every project are on the guidelines page: two randomly assigned conditions, a manipulation check, a dependent variable with real variation, at least one moderator measured before and one mediator measured after the manipulation, hypotheses stated before data collection, and about 120 respondents. If none of the topics fits, you may propose your own — it must follow the same template and come with a starter paper from a comparable journal; discuss it with us before Coaching I.

Artificial intelligence

1. Human-made or AI-made? Judging creative work

Why it matters. Generative AI now produces ad copy, product designs, music, and images at almost no cost. The open question is not whether AI can create, but how customers react when they learn that it did: in a field test with the fashion house Missoni, AI-recommended designs outsold the designs chosen by human experts — yet disclosing that AI was involved lowered what customers were willing to pay.

Your task. Does telling customers that a piece of creative work (an ad, a design, a text) was made with AI change how they evaluate and value it?

Starter literature. Moreau, P., Prandelli, E., & Schreier, M. (2023). Generative artificial intelligence and design co-creation in luxury new product development: The power of discarded ideas. Working paper, Bocconi University / WU Vienna. https://dx.doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4630856 · Zhang, Y., & Gosline, R. (2023). Human favoritism, not AI aversion: People’s perceptions (and bias) toward generative AI, human experts, and human–GAI collaboration in persuasive content generation. Judgment and Decision Making, 18, e41. https://doi.org/10.1017/jdm.2023.37

2. “Made with AI”: disclosure labels on social media

Why it matters. TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube label AI-generated content, and the EU AI Act requires the disclosure of synthetic media. Analyzing about one million TikTok posts before and after the platform’s mandatory label, and in a series of experiments, Carney, Riveros, and Tully find that the label reduces engagement — with the post and with the creator.

Your task. How does an AI disclosure on a creator’s or a brand’s post affect how people engage with the post and with its author?

Starter literature. Carney, S., Riveros, I., & Tully, S. M. (2026). Made with AI: Consumer engagement with social media containing AI disclosures. Journal of Consumer Research, forthcoming. (MSI Working Paper 24-154: https://msi.org/working-paper/made-with-ai-consumer-engagement-with-media-containing-ai-disclosures)

3. Recommended by an algorithm or by a person? The word-of-machine effect

Why it matters. Shopping assistants, playlist curators, and “customers like you” modules all hand out recommendations — some presented as coming from an algorithm, some from a human expert. Longoni and Cian show that whether consumers follow them depends systematically on what the consumer is looking for in the product.

Your task. When do consumers follow a recommendation from an AI system rather than from a human expert — and for which products or purchase situations is one source more persuasive than the other?

Starter literature. Longoni, C., & Cian, L. (2022). Artificial intelligence in utilitarian vs. hedonic contexts: The “word-of-machine” effect. Journal of Marketing, 86(1), 91–108. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242920957347

4. Chatbots in customer service: should the bot reveal itself?

Why it matters. Most companies now route service requests through AI agents. In a field experiment with more than 6,000 sales calls, Luo and colleagues found that an undisclosed AI agent sold as well as experienced employees — but telling customers before the conversation that they were talking to a bot cut purchases by about 80%, although the conversation itself was identical. Later work maps out when consumers react negatively to service bots and when they do not.

Your task. Should a company reveal that a service interaction is handled by an AI agent — and what does the disclosure do to customers’ satisfaction, trust, and loyalty?

Starter literature. Luo, X., Tong, S., Fang, Z., & Qu, Z. (2019). Frontiers: Machines vs. humans: The impact of artificial intelligence chatbot disclosure on customer purchases. Marketing Science, 38(6), 937–947. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2019.1192 · Castelo, N., Boegershausen, J., Hildebrand, C., & Henkel, A. P. (2023). Understanding and improving consumer reactions to service bots. Journal of Consumer Research, 50(4), 848–863. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucad023 · Crolic, C., Thomaz, F., Hadi, R., & Stephen, A. T. (2022). Blame the bot: Anthropomorphism and anger in customer–chatbot interactions. Journal of Marketing, 86(1), 132–148. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429211045687

5. Virtual influencers

Why it matters. Computer-generated influencers such as Lil Miquela have millions of followers, never miss a deadline, and never cause a scandal — but do their recommendations work? Zhou, Yan, and Jiang show that virtual influencers are not equally effective for every kind of product, and Cascio Rizzo, Villarroel Ordenes, and Berger show that how they are presented changes how much followers engage with them.

Your task. Are virtual influencers as effective as human influencers in promoting a product — and what determines whether they are?

Starter literature. Zhou, X., Yan, X., & Jiang, Y. (2024). Making sense? The sensory-specific nature of virtual influencer effectiveness. Journal of Marketing, 88(4), 84–106. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429231203699 · Cascio Rizzo, G. L., Villarroel Ordenes, F., & Berger, J. (2026). What drives virtual influencers’ impact? Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, forthcoming. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-026-01185-x

Sustainability and pro-social behavior

6. “Ugly” produce and food waste

Why it matters. Up to a third of fruit and vegetables never reach a shelf because they look odd. Retailers hesitate to call such produce “ugly” — most managers prefer “imperfect” or no label at all. Mookerjee, Cornil, and Hoegg show that an honest “Ugly” label can increase purchases of unattractive produce.

Your task. How should a retailer label and price imperfect produce so that customers buy it?

Starter literature. Mookerjee, S., Cornil, Y., & Hoegg, J. (2021). From waste to taste: How “ugly” labels can increase purchase of unattractive produce. Journal of Marketing, 85(3), 62–77. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242920988656

7. Cause-related marketing: “we donate €1” vs. “we plant a tree”

Why it matters. Linking a purchase to a donation is one of the most common ways to differentiate a product. A meta-analysis of more than 240 studies by Schamp and colleagues shows that how the donation is presented matters as much as its size.

Your task. How should a brand frame and communicate a cause-related donation so that it has the largest effect on customers’ choices?

Starter literature. Schamp, C., Heitmann, M., Bijmolt, T. H. A., & Katzenstein, R. (2023). The effectiveness of cause-related marketing: A meta-analysis on consumer responses. Journal of Marketing Research, 60(1), 189–215. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221109782

8. Getting donors to talk about their giving

Why it matters. Word of mouth is the cheapest marketing a charity can get, but donors rarely tell others that they gave. In a field experiment with thousands of donors, Silver and Small show that the way a charity asks donors to share makes a large difference.

Your task. How should a charity ask its donors to share their donation so that they actually do?

Starter literature. Silver, I., & Small, D. A. (2024). Put your mouth where your money is: A field experiment encouraging donors to share about charity. Marketing Science, 43(2), 392–406. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2023.1450

9. Showing what recycling makes

Why it matters. Recycling campaigns usually tell people to recycle. Winterich, Nenkov, and Gonzales show that telling them what their waste becomes works better — in the lab, in online ads, and in the field.

Your task. How should a recycling campaign (or the marketing of products made from recycled materials) communicate, so that people recycle more or choose the recycled product?

Starter literature. Winterich, K. P., Nenkov, G. Y., & Gonzales, G. E. (2019). Knowing what it makes: How product transformation salience increases recycling. Journal of Marketing, 83(4), 21–37. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242919842167

Authenticity, brands, and influencers

10. Brand activism: when taking a stand pays off

Why it matters. From Nike to Ben & Jerry’s, brands take positions on social and political issues — and get accused of “woke washing” when the stance looks opportunistic. Mukherjee and Althuizen show that activism can hurt a brand as well as help it, depending on the audience and on how the stance is perceived.

Your task. When does taking a public stand on a social issue help a brand, and when does it hurt?

Starter literature. Mukherjee, S., & Althuizen, N. (2020). Brand activism: Does courting controversy help or hurt a brand? International Journal of Research in Marketing, 37(4), 772–788. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2020.02.008

11. The handmade effect

Why it matters. “Handmade” sells — on Etsy, at Christmas markets, in luxury. Fuchs, Schreier, and van Osselaer show that consumers find handmade products more attractive and are willing to pay more for them, and they explain why — and when the effect disappears.

Your task. Does describing a product as handmade make it more attractive and more valuable to customers — and under which circumstances does it not?

Starter literature. Fuchs, C., Schreier, M., & van Osselaer, S. M. J. (2015). The handmade effect: What’s love got to do with it? Journal of Marketing, 79(2), 98–110. https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.14.0018

12. “Phresh” or “Fresh”? Unconventionally spelled brand names

Why it matters. Lyft, Tumblr, Froot Loops: misspelled brand names are supposed to be memorable and cool. Across eight experiments, Costello, Walker, and Reczek find that they mostly backfire — with some exceptions that matter for naming decisions.

Your task. Does an unconventionally spelled name help or hurt a new brand?

Starter literature. Costello, J. P., Walker, J., & Reczek, R. W. (2023). “Choozing” the best spelling: Consumer response to unconventionally spelled brand names. Journal of Marketing, 87(6), 889–905. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429231162367

13. Influencer marketing: does the #ad disclosure hurt?

Why it matters. Sponsored posts must be labeled as advertising, and brands fear that the label destroys the influencer’s credibility. Karagür and colleagues show that disclosure is a double-edged sword whose net effect depends on how it is done and on the audience.

Your task. Does disclosing a sponsorship reduce the effectiveness of an influencer’s post — and does it matter how the sponsorship is disclosed?

Starter literature. Karagür, Z., Becker, J.-M., Klein, K., & Edeling, A. (2022). How, why, and when disclosure type matters for influencer marketing. International Journal of Research in Marketing, 39(2), 313–335. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.09.006 · A related alternative: Valsesia, F., Proserpio, D., & Nunes, J. C. (2020). The positive effect of not following others on social media. Journal of Marketing Research, 57(6), 1152–1168. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243720915467

14. “This product is not for everyone”: dissuasive framing

Why it matters. Conventional copy tells customers why a product is right for them. Dissuasive framing tells the others that it is not (“If you don’t like strong coffee, this is not the coffee for you”). Wallach, Blair, and Tanenbaum find that this counterintuitive approach can make a product more attractive to the customers it is meant for.

Your task. Does telling consumers whom a product is not for make it more attractive to its target customers — and what does it do to everyone else?

Starter literature. Wallach, K. A., Blair, S., & Tanenbaum, J. L. (2026). This article is not for everyone: The impact of dissuasive framing on consumer response to product messages. Journal of Consumer Research, 53(3), 490–511. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaf034

15. Inclusive advertising: people with disabilities in ads

Why it matters. Brands increasingly feature people with disabilities in their advertising, and many managers fear a backlash. Across nine preregistered studies, Cossu, Estes, and Vosgerau find a robust positive effect on how consumers evaluate the ad, the brand, and the product — with boundary conditions that matter for how such ads are made.

Your task. How do consumers respond to ads that feature people with disabilities, and what should brands keep in mind when they do it?

Starter literature. Cossu, M., Estes, Z., & Vosgerau, J. (2026). Beyond visibility: The disability inclusion effect in advertising. Journal of Marketing, forthcoming. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429261447790 · Background: Campbell, C., Sands, S., McFerran, B., & Mavrommatis, A. (2024). Diversity representation in advertising. Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 52(1), 1–19. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-023-00994-8

Pricing, numbers, and packaging

16. Shrinkflation vs. a price increase

Why it matters. When costs rise, many manufacturers shrink the pack instead of raising the price. Consumers notice — “shrinkflation” became a political issue in Europe, and France and Hungary now require labels. Evangelidis shows that consumers judge a size decrease and an economically equivalent price increase very differently.

Your task. When costs rise, should a brand raise the price or reduce the pack size — and how do customers react to each?

Starter literature. Evangelidis, I. (2024). Frontiers: Shrinkflation aversion: When and why product size decreases are seen as more unfair than equivalent price increases. Marketing Science, 43(2), 280–288. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2023.0269

17. Paid upgrades: paying twice for a feature you already own

Why it matters. Heated seats as a subscription, a camera feature unlocked for a fee: manufacturers increasingly build features into the product and charge later for activating them. Garbas and colleagues show that such internal upgrades are evaluated very differently from conventional add-ons at the same price.

Your task. How do customers react when they are asked to pay for activating a feature that is already built into their product, compared with buying an add-on — and what does that mean for the design of upgrade offers?

Starter literature. Garbas, J., Schubach, S., Mende, M., Scott, M. L., & Schumann, J. H. (2023). You want to sell this to me twice!? How perceptions of betrayal may undermine internal product upgrades. Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 51(2), 286–309. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-022-00881-8

18. Stars or numbers? The format of customer ratings

Why it matters. Amazon shows stars, Google shows a number, many sites show both. Abell, Morgan, and Romero show that the format in which the same average rating is displayed changes what customers perceive — and what they buy.

Your task. How should an online retailer display average customer ratings, and does the format change how consumers evaluate and choose products?

Starter literature. Abell, A., Morgan, C., & Romero, M. (2024). The power of a star rating: Differential effects of customer rating formats on magnitude perceptions and consumer reactions. Journal of Marketing Research, 61(6), 1041–1057. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437241240694

19. “Only 7 ingredients”: fewer is more natural

Why it matters. “Clean label” claims are everywhere. Kim and colleagues show that framing a product as having few ingredients changes preferences even when the ingredient list is identical — in a field test, a single added line raised click-through rates by 44%.

Your task. Does highlighting a small number of ingredients make a food product more attractive — and when does it not?

Starter literature. Kim, M. Y., Gershon, R., Scott, S. E., Kupor, D., Chen, T., & Trudel, R. (2026). Less is more (natural): The effect of ingredient quantity framing on consumer preferences. Journal of Marketing Research, forthcoming. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437261432622

20. Transparent packaging

Why it matters. A window in the box lets customers see the product — and, as Marckhgott, Kamleitner (WU), and Estes show across seven studies, this changes how much they want it. The effect has boundary conditions that packaging designers need to know.

Your task. Does transparent packaging make a product more attractive than opaque packaging — and when?

Starter literature. Marckhgott, E., Kamleitner, B., & Estes, Z. (2026). A window of opportunity: Transparent packaging affects product preference via psychological ownership. International Journal of Research in Marketing, 43(3), 929–946. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2026.01.008

Lifting the veil: The benefits of cost transparency. Marketing Science, 39(6), 1105–1121. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2019.1200 · Wilken, R., Schmitt, J., Dost, F., et al. (2024). Does the presentation of true costs at the point of purchase nudge consumers toward sustainable product options? Marketing Letters, 35, 589–602. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-023-09713-3
  • Digital tipping prompts. How do suggested tip amounts on the card terminal affect tipping and the customer’s experience? Alexander, D., Boone, C., & Lynn, M. (2021). The effects of tip recommendations on customer tipping, satisfaction, repatronage, and spending. Management Science, 67(1), 146–165. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3541
  • Front-of-package nutrition labels. Which label format changes food choices? Ikonen, I., Sotgiu, F., Aydinli, A., & Verlegh, P. W. J. (2020). Consumer effects of front-of-package nutrition labeling: An interdisciplinary meta-analysis. Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, 48, 360–383. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-019-00663-9
