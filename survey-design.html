Survey design

📽 Slides for this module · PDF handout · 💻 R code

The example study: 📋 the survey to import into Qualtrics (.qsf) · 📄 the raw data (a Qualtrics export) · 🎤 the final presentation (PDF)

Driving question: How do you turn a causal question into a questionnaire that can answer it?

In Chapters 2 to 6, somebody else had collected the data: you received a file and analyzed it. In the group project, the direction is reversed — you produce the data. This changes the price of a mistake. A wrong line of R code is corrected in a minute. A moderator that was asked after the stimulus, a randomizer that showed both versions to everyone, or an outcome that hardly varies cannot be repaired once 120 people have answered. Analysis problems can be solved in January; design problems have to be solved now.

This page is the manual for Part 1 of the group project: a between-subjects experiment with two conditions, one moderator, and one mediator, built into a questionnaire in Qualtrics. It is organized as seven steps (Table 1), and one example study runs through all of them: A seal for the coffee. The online roaster whose storytelling experiment you analyzed in the coding block of 6  Mechanisms & Heterogeneity has a new question: does a sustainability seal on the product page pay off? You follow the study from the first idea to the raw Qualtrics export of about 140 respondents, with everything that a real export contains: previews, unfinished questionnaires, and typos. (The data are simulated for this course; the file has the format of a real export.) The storytelling study returns once, in Step 2 — as an example of a manipulation that changes more than one thing.

After working through this page you will be able to:

  • Translate a managerial question into a blueprint with two conditions, an outcome, a moderator, a mediator, hypotheses stated in advance, and a sample size that can detect the effect you expect
  • Design a manipulation in which the two versions differ in exactly one respect — and check whether respondents noticed it
  • Choose measures with enough variation and reliability: a dependent variable in euros or on a wide scale, single questions for facts, and reflective multi-item scales from the literature for abstract constructs — and assess such a scale with Cronbach’s alpha
  • Put the blocks of a questionnaire into the order that the causal logic requires
  • Set up the randomizer in the Qualtrics survey flow so that each respondent sees exactly one version and the condition is stored in the data — and recognize the typical mistakes
  • Pretest, field, and export a survey, and turn the raw Qualtrics file into the data set for your analyses

Session 8 covers all seven steps. For the draft that you present in Coaching I you need Steps 1 to 5; Step 6 guides the data collection in December; Step 7 is the template for your analysis and for Coaching II, and it ends with an example of the final presentation.

Step 1 · Plan: start from the end

Every column is a decision

The most common mistake happens before the first question is written: groups collect questions that “would be interesting to ask” and decide afterwards what to do with the answers. Work in the opposite direction. You already know the analyses you will run, because every project answers the same three questions: Does it work? For whom? Why? Each question corresponds to a model that you know from 4  Learning from Experiments and 6  Mechanisms & Heterogeneity; each model needs certain columns in the data; each column is produced by a question — or by the randomizer — at a particular place in the questionnaire (Table 2).

This is the data set that the coffee study delivers in the end — one row per respondent, and every column a decision that was made before the first respondent clicked on the link:

Design backwards, build forwards: plan from the analysis back to the questions (this step), then build the questionnaire in the order in which respondents will go through it (Steps 2 to 5).

The causal diagram of your study

Every project has the same causal diagram — the full cast of Variables and their roles, without the confounder, because the randomizer takes care of it (Figure 1). In the coffee study, the roaster Sierra Verde considers having its coffee certified. A certification costs money, so the roaster wants to know whether a sustainability seal on the product page (the treatment X) raises customers’ willingness to pay (the outcome Y). The team expects the seal to work because buying a certified coffee promises a good feeling — the warm glow of doing something good (the mediator M) — and to work better for people who are concerned about the environment (environmental concern, the moderator W).

The mediator is a choice — and it decides what the manager learns

A seal could raise willingness to pay for more than one reason, and each reason leads to a different decision (Table 3).

The coffee study measures one of these mechanisms, warm glow: the team considers it the most plausible one, and a short published scale exists for it. This choice limits what the study can say. A large indirect effect through warm glow tells the roaster how to use the seal; whether expected quality or credibility play a role as well remains untested. The mediator turns “it works” into “what to do next” — which is why it deserves as much thought as the manipulation. For your own project, take the mechanism from your starter paper, measure one mediator well rather than three badly, and be prepared to say in the coaching which alternative explanations you did not measure.

The blueprint

Before you write a single question, fill in a one-page blueprint (Table 4). It forces the decisions that are expensive to change later, and it is the first slide of your Coaching I presentation.

Three remarks on filling it in:

  • Use the starter paper as a quarry. The method section of your starter paper tells you how the authors manipulated the treatment; the measures section or the web appendix lists the items of their scales. You do not replicate the paper — but you should not invent a manipulation or a scale that somebody else has already developed and validated.
  • State the direction. “Environmental concern moderates the effect” is not a hypothesis; “the effect is larger for respondents with higher environmental concern, because a seal means more to people who care about what it certifies” is one — a similar pattern has been found in a tasting study with “eco-friendly” coffee (Sörqvist et al. 2013), and you have seen it for the shipping label in the coding block of 6  Mechanisms & Heterogeneity. If you cannot say in which direction a moderator works and why, choose a different one.
  • Fix the plan before you see data. Hypotheses, the main analysis, and the exclusion rules are written down now. Everything you discover later is welcome — as an exploratory finding, labeled as such (Using p-values responsibly, The pitfalls of looking for heterogeneity).

How many respondents do you need?

One row of the blueprint is a calculation, and you know it from How many users do we need?: the number of respondents follows from the difference that the study should be able to detect. It needs two numbers.

  • The difference between the two conditions that you expect, in the unit of your outcome. Ask which difference would still matter for the decision, or take the evidence of earlier studies — with caution, because published effects tend to be overestimated (Using p-values responsibly). The coffee team plans for 1 euro: a smaller premium would hardly justify a certification, and the storytelling study, with its +1.66 euros, suggests that a difference of this size is realistic.
  • The standard deviation of the outcome: how much do respondents differ from each other in their answers? In the storytelling study, the willingness to pay for the same pack of coffee varied with a standard deviation of about 2 euros. If you have no earlier data, ask ten people your outcome question. For a multi-item scale with seven points, a standard deviation between 1 and 1.5 scale points is typical (in the coffee study: 1.1 for the warm glow scale, 1.3 for environmental concern).

Do this calculation for your own project and bring it to Coaching I. If it asks for many more respondents than your group can recruit, do not hope for the best — change the design: make the manipulation stronger (Step 2), measure the outcome more precisely (Step 3: Figure 4 shows how much the measure matters), and measure a control variable before the stimulus that explains a part of the outcome (Motivation: the average effect hides the interesting part). And keep in mind what the calculation covers: it refers to H1. The interaction of H2 is estimated far less precisely than a difference between two groups (Moderation and mediation in your group project) — plan the moderation as a hypothesis whose direction you can examine, not as a result you can count on.

Step 2 · Manipulate: two versions, one difference

The manipulation is the only thing in your study that you control completely, and it defines what your result means. If the two versions differ in one respect, a difference in the outcome can be attributed to it. If they differ in two, you will never know which one was responsible. Figure 2 shows the two versions of the coffee study: the same product page, without and with the seal.

What makes a good manipulation

One difference. Describe the difference between your two versions in one short sentence: the product page shows a seal for certified sustainable farming — or it does not. If you need the word “and”, the manipulation is confounded. The cleanest manipulations add, remove, or exchange one element of an otherwise identical stimulus: a seal, a label, a disclosure, a claim, the format of a number. Build one version, copy it, and change this one element. What would go wrong otherwise: a seal and a more attractive photo (then the “seal effect” may be a photo effect), a seal and an additional paragraph about the farm’s environmental program (an effect of length and information), or a different coffee in each version (a product effect).

Strong enough to be seen. With 60 respondents per condition, you can only detect medium-to-large effects — a standardized difference of about d = 0.5 (How many users do we need?; the calculation is in Step 1). This is the price of a clean manipulation: a single element is easily overlooked. A small logo in a corner of the page will most likely end in “not significant”, whatever the truth is. Make the one element impossible to miss — the seal of the coffee study is large, sits on the product picture, and says in words what it certifies — and check afterwards whether respondents noticed it.

Realistic. The stimulus should look like the real thing: a product page, an ad, a social-media post, a package. A slide, a mock-up tool, or an image generator is sufficient to build one — as long as everything except the manipulated element is identical in both versions. A fictitious brand — and here also a fictitious seal — keeps respondents from bringing their opinions about a real brand or a real certification into the study; this removes noise, at the price of some realism. If you describe a situation in words (“Imagine you are looking for …”), describe everything a respondent needs to answer the later questions — otherwise each respondent fills the gaps differently.

One version per respondent. The project uses a between-subjects design (Randomization): the randomizer shows each respondent exactly one version. Respondents who see both versions compare them, guess what you are after, and answer accordingly.

On one page, impossible to skip. Put the stimulus on its own page, before any question about it. In Qualtrics, a Timing question on the same page records how long respondents stay on it and can hold back the “next” button for a few seconds.

A closer look at the storytelling study: one difference?

In the coding block of 6  Mechanisms & Heterogeneity you analyzed a study by the same roaster: the product description either listed the facts about the coffee or told its story. Figure 3 shows what the respondents of that study saw. Describe the difference between the two versions in one sentence — and then look again.

The two texts were written to carry the same information: origin, altitude, variety, harvest by hand, washing, a family farm, weekly roasting, tasting notes. But the Story version does more than change the form. It introduces a person with a name and a daily routine (Rosa Medina, every morning at five, the steep slopes). It adds details that the Facts version does not contain (two sons, a grandfather who planted the first trees). It speaks with a different voice (“we roast her harvest”). And it is six words longer (69 instead of 63). None of this is carelessness — a story cannot be told without a protagonist and a sequence of events. The manipulation is a bundle.

What follows? The experiment remains valid: the randomizer made the two groups of respondents comparable, so the difference of 1.66 euros that you estimated there was caused by the version of the page. But “the version” is everything in which the two pages differ. The study cannot say whether the effect is due to the narrative form, the named farmer, the additional details, or the personal voice — and a manager who wants to apply the result does not know which of them to copy. Randomization makes the respondents of the two groups comparable. It does nothing for the stimuli; making those comparable is your job.

Some treatments are bundles by nature: a story, a tone of voice, a virtual instead of a human influencer, an ad that shows a different person. If your topic is of this kind, three things help:

  • Hold constant whatever can be held constant — the same facts in both versions (the Facts version could name the farmer, too), the same length, the same picture, the same layout.
  • Say what the versions differ in, and interpret the result as the effect of this bundle, not of one of its ingredients.
  • Check both sides: a manipulation check for the difference you intended, and a second question for a difference that should not be there (for example “The description was easy to understand”, which should be answered alike in both versions).

Where you have the choice, choose the manipulation with one element — as the coffee study of this page does.

The manipulation check

A manipulation check measures whether respondents perceived the difference you built in: “The coffee on the product page carried a seal for sustainable farming” (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). It protects you in the worst case: if the outcome does not differ between the conditions, the check tells you whether the idea was wrong (respondents saw the seal, but it did not matter) or the execution was too weak (they did not see it). Three rules:

  1. Check the manipulation, not the hypothesis. Ask whether respondents noticed what you varied (the seal), not whether it had the effect you hoped for (a good feeling — that is the mediator).
  2. Ask it after the outcome. A manipulation check is itself a small intervention: it draws attention to what you varied and can strengthen or change its effect on the questions that follow (Hauser et al. 2018). Placed after the dependent variable, it can no longer do that.
  3. Report it — do not use it to exclude respondents. Dropping respondents who “failed” the check seems to clean the data, but it breaks the randomization: who passes the check depends on the condition, so the remaining groups are no longer comparable (Aronow et al. 2019). The coding block of Step 7 shows this with the coffee data.

If your stimulus contains invented information — a brand or a seal that does not exist, an ad that was not made by an AI — say so on the last page of the questionnaire (a debriefing).

Step 3 · Measure: from constructs to questions

“Willingness to pay”, “warm glow”, and “environmental concern” are constructs: ideas in the heads of researchers. Respondents answer questions. Measurement is the translation from one to the other, and it decides whether your analysis can find what your design created. Table 6 is the measurement plan of the coffee study: for each variable of the blueprint the question and its source, the answer format, and the name of the column in the data.

These are twelve questions. Your questionnaire will have about 30: the additional ones describe your respondents and their behavior in the product category (what they buy, where, how often), which makes your report richer and gives you further control variables.

The dependent variable needs real variation

The level of measurement you choose now decides which analyses are possible later (Variables and levels of measurement) — and how much your 120 respondents can tell you. Good dependent variables can take many values:

  • Willingness to pay in euros — an open question with a numeric answer. It is a ratio scale, managers understand it immediately, and it connects to a pricing decision. Stated willingness to pay is usually higher than what people pay when real money is at stake; but both of your groups overstate in the same way, and what you interpret is the difference between them. In a comparison with real purchases, even the simple open question — although it overstated the level — could still lead to the right pricing decision (Miller et al. 2011).
  • A likelihood from 0 to 100 — “How likely are you to buy this product? (0 = certainly not, 100 = certainly)”, as a slider or a number.
  • A multi-item scale — e.g., three purchase-intention statements on a 7-point scale, averaged.

A yes/no question (“Would you buy this product?”) and a single rating question are weak dependent variables for a study of this size. Figure 4 shows why: the same true effect and the same 120 respondents, measured in four ways.

With a yes/no question, the experiment detects the effect in fewer than half of all cases; with a scale built from three questions, in about two of three. A coarse or noisy measure is like throwing respondents away. If the managerial question is about a yes/no decision, ask the choice in addition to a finer measure, not instead of it.

Finally, give respondents what they need to answer. If they cannot be expected to know typical prices in the category, state a reference (“A 250 g pack of coffee in the supermarket costs about 5 to 7 euros”) — in both conditions, in exactly the same words.

Scales for the mediator and the moderator

Attitudes and perceptions are measured with itemized rating scales. Two formats cover almost everything you need. A Likert scale asks for the agreement with statements; the coffee study uses it for the mediator and for the moderator (Table 7, Table 8). A semantic differential places the answer between two opposite adjectives; it is the usual format for evaluations of a product, a brand, or an ad (Table 9).

The measurement plan thus contains two kinds of measures: single questions for facts and concrete judgments (cups of coffee, willingness to pay, the manipulation check, age), and scales of three items for the two abstract constructs. Why three statements for one construct, and not one? The next four sections give the answer: what a single answer contains, when one question is enough, which kind of multi-item measure you are building, and how its reliability is assessed.

Every answer contains error: reliability and validity

No question measures a construct perfectly. In the classic model of measurement, every observed answer consists of three parts:

\[ X_O = X_T + X_S + X_R \]

the true value \(X_T\) of the characteristic you want to measure, a systematic error \(X_S\) that pushes the answers in one direction — a leading question, an unbalanced answer scale, the wish to appear in a good light — and a random error \(X_R\) that differs from answer to answer: a word understood differently, a distraction, the mood of the moment. Two quality criteria follow from this model (Malhotra 2020, chap. 9):

  • Reliability is consistency: a reliable measure gives nearly the same result when it is repeated under the same conditions. There is little random error.
  • Validity is truthfulness: a valid measure captures what it is supposed to capture, so that differences in the scores reflect true differences between respondents. There is neither systematic nor random error.

Figure 5 shows the two criteria with the picture of a target. A measure can be reliable without being valid: a bathroom scale that is set two kilograms too high shows the same wrong weight every morning. The reverse is not possible — answers that scatter at random cannot be on target. Reliability is a necessary, but not a sufficient condition for validity.

In your project, the two criteria are secured in different ways. Validity is decided when you choose and word the questions: use established scales, check that every item really belongs to the construct, and avoid the wording problems described at the end of this step (wording rules). Reliability can be built into the questionnaire — by asking several questions about the same construct — and it can be checked with your data.

One question or several?

A construct is an abstract concept — environmental concern, warm glow, satisfaction — that cannot be observed directly; it is latent. What you observe are the answers to items: single questions or statements. A multi-item scale combines the answers to several items into one score, usually their mean.

Whether one item is enough depends on what you measure (Table 10). Facts and concrete judgments — age, the number of cups per day, a willingness to pay, “How much do you like this ad?” — are understood in the same way by everybody, and one good question captures them (Bergkvist and Rossiter 2007). Abstract constructs have several facets and no single natural wording. Here every single answer contains a good deal of random error, and scales with several items do better under most conditions (Diamantopoulos et al. 2012). How far the use of single items should go is still debated (Kamakura 2015; Bergkvist 2015). For your project the rule is simple: single questions for facts and concrete judgments, and three items each for the mediator and the moderator — a compromise between reliability and length.

What would the coffee study lose with one question? The study measures anticipated warm glow with three statements. Suppose it had asked only one of them. Figure 6 plots the answers to the first statement against the mean of the other two: the same respondents, the same construct, the same minute — and a correlation of only r = .61. A large part of every single answer is noise.

The noise has consequences (Table 11). Averaging over three statements cancels a part of the random error — the logic by which the mean of many respondents is more precise than a single one (The standard error), applied to questions. With one statement instead of three, the seal seems to change the warm glow less (measured in standard deviations of the answers), warm glow is less closely related to willingness to pay, and the mediation analysis finds an indirect effect of only 0.48 to 0.72 euros instead of 1.03. Nothing about the respondents has changed, only the precision of the measurement. Random error in a variable weakens its relationships with all other variables; in a mediation analysis, an unreliable mediator makes the mechanism look weaker than it is.

Reflective or formative?

Measures with several items come in two kinds. The difference lies in the direction of the arrow between the construct and its items, and the standard illustration is the degree of drunkenness (Figure 7). You can measure it through its causes — the number of beers and spirits, the time since the last drink — or through its effects: unclear speech, a slow reaction, an unsteady walk.

  • In a reflective measurement, the items are effects of the construct. Because buying the coffee promises a respondent a good feeling, she agrees that she can feel good because she helps to protect the environment, that she can feel better because she does not harm it, and that she has the feeling of contributing to the well-being of humanity and nature. The items are interchangeable expressions of the same thing: they must be correlated, and dropping one of them does not change what is measured. Additional items increase the reliability. The result is called a scale.
  • In a formative measurement, the items are causes or components of the construct. The daily coffee consumption is made up of the cups at home, at work, and in cafés. The items are not interchangeable and need not be correlated — somebody who drinks all their coffee at work is not measured badly. Dropping an item changes the meaning of the construct. Additional items increase the validity, because they cover more of the construct. The result is called an index.

Figure 8 shows these two examples from the world of the coffee study, and Table 12 summarizes how to tell the two kinds apart and what follows from the answer (Jarvis et al. 2003; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001).

The attitudes and perceptions that serve as mediators and moderators in your projects — warm glow, trust, authenticity, involvement, attitudes toward AI or toward sustainability — are reflective constructs, and the published scales for them are reflective scales. Formative indices typically describe behavior or knowledge: how intensively somebody uses AI tools (chatbots, image generators, AI features in apps), or how sustainably somebody lives (recycling, organic food, public transport). If you build such an index, list its components first, and do not expect — or require — them to be correlated. Treating a formative index like a reflective scale, for example by deleting the items that correlate weakly with the others, is a common mistake in marketing research (Jarvis et al. 2003).

Cronbach’s alpha: the reliability of a reflective scale

If the items of a reflective scale express the same construct, the answers to them must go together. Cronbach’s alpha (Cronbach 1951) turns this idea into a number. For a scale with \(k\) items and a mean correlation of \(\bar{r}\) between the items, it is

\[ \alpha = \frac{k \, \bar{r}}{1 + (k - 1) \, \bar{r}} \]

For items that are positively correlated, alpha lies between 0 and 1. It estimates the share of the variance of the scale score that is not random error, and is thus a measure of reliability — more precisely, of the internal consistency of the items. (A negative alpha is a warning sign: an item is coded in the wrong direction.) Two things make it larger (Table 13): stronger correlations between the items, and more items.

In the coffee study, the three items on environmental concern correlate with a mean of 0.60, which gives an alpha of 0.82; for warm glow, the mean correlation is 0.53 and alpha is 0.77. Step 7 shows how to compute these numbers in R. How to read them:

  • Conventions. By a widely used convention, values of about 0.7 or more are acceptable and values of 0.8 or more are good; Malhotra (2020, chap. 9) calls a value of 0.6 or less unsatisfactory. For a scale with three items, 0.7 is a realistic target.
  • Higher is not always better. An alpha above 0.95 usually means that the items ask the same question in slightly different words.
  • Alpha depends on the number of items. Six items that correlate at 0.3 reach about the same alpha as three items that correlate at 0.5 (Table 13). Do not compare scales of different length by their alpha alone.
  • Alpha is not validity. It tells you that the items measure the same thing, not that they measure the right thing.
  • Alpha is for reflective scales only. The components of a formative index need not be correlated; a low alpha says nothing about its quality.
  • Recode reverse-scored items first. Otherwise they correlate negatively with the other items, and alpha collapses (Step 7).

The formula uses correlations, that is, standardized items. psych::alpha() reports this value as std.alpha and, in addition, a raw_alpha that is computed from the covariances of the items as they are; for the items of one rating scale the two hardly differ.

Where the items come from — and six decisions

Where do the items come from? From the literature, not from your imagination. Look in the measures section or the appendix of your starter paper, in the papers it cites, and in collections such as the Marketing Scales Handbook (Bruner 2019). Adapt the wording to your product, keep the answer format of the original, and cite the source on your slides. Scales that have been validated measure what they claim to measure — and nobody will ask you in the coaching why you worded an item the way you did.

Decisions for every rating scale. Use seven points (five are acceptable; more points give more variation). Label at least the two end points, and use the same labels and the same direction for all items of a scale. An odd number of points allows a neutral answer. Offer “don’t know” only if respondents may really not know (e.g., a brand they have never heard of). Avoid negations (“not”, “un-”) in statements of your own; if a validated scale contains a reverse-scored item — like the second item on environmental concern — keep it, and remember to recode it before you compute the scale (Step 7). On a phone, a matrix with more than five statements becomes hard to read.

Controls, attention check, demographics

  • Control variables are measured before the stimulus. In a randomized experiment they are not needed against confounding, but they make the estimate more precise (Motivation: the average effect hides the interesting part), and they are the confounders of the mediator–outcome relationship that mediation analysis has to adjust for (Mediation: why does it work?). Good candidates predict the outcome: how often respondents buy the product, how much they usually pay, how much they know about the category.
  • One attention check, also before the stimulus: a question with an obvious instructed answer. The rule for excluding respondents who fail it is part of your blueprint.
  • Demographics come last: age as a number, gender with inclusive categories and “prefer not to say”, and whatever else describes your sample (occupation, country). Ask for sensitive information such as income only if you need it — in categories, never as an exact number.

Wording: eight rules

NoteUnder the hood: other question types

Qualtrics offers many more question types than this design needs. Three are useful for the descriptive part of your questionnaire. Multiple choice with multiple answers (“Which of these brands do you know?”) gives one 0/1 column per option and is a good warm-up question. A rank order question (“Rank these five product features”) yields ordinal data: you learn the order, not the distances. A constant sum question (“Distribute 100 points among these features”) also gives the distances, at the cost of more effort for the respondent. Use them to describe your market — the experiment itself needs none of them. The textbook treatment of scaling techniques and questionnaire design is Malhotra (2020, chap. 8 to 10).

Step 4 · Order: the questionnaire follows the causal diagram

Where a question stands decides what its answers can be used for. The principle fits in one sentence: everything that is asked after the stimulus can be changed by it. A variable that the stimulus may have changed cannot serve as a moderator, as a control variable, or as a criterion for excluding respondents — conditioning on such a variable breaks the comparison that the randomizer created (Montgomery et al. 2018). A variable that the stimulus should change — the mediator, the outcome — must come after it. The causal diagram of Figure 1 therefore dictates the order of the questionnaire (Figure 9).

Three questions come up every year:

  • “Can we ask the moderator at the end, so that it does not influence how respondents see the stimulus?” No — a moderator that is measured after the stimulus can be changed by it: after seeing a coffee with a sustainability seal, some respondents will express more concern for the environment. The worry behind the question is legitimate, but the evidence is reassuring (see the box below). Stable characteristics that a stimulus cannot change — age, gender — can stay at the end.
  • “Should the demographics not come first, as in every form?” No — they are the least interesting questions of your survey, and the first minute decides whether a respondent stays.
  • “Why is the manipulation check not directly after the stimulus?” Because it would tell respondents what the study is about, just before the questions that matter (Step 2).
NoteUnder the hood: three ordering questions that researchers debate

Does a moderator that is asked first prime respondents? Asking about environmental concern, attitudes, or identity directly before a stimulus might change how respondents react to it. Measuring the moderator afterwards avoids this, but risks that the stimulus changes the moderator — the trade-off between priming bias and post-treatment bias is analyzed formally by Blackwell et al. (2025). The empirical evidence favors measuring first: across six experiments, Sheagley and Clifford (2025) found little evidence that measuring a moderator changes the treatment effect, even when it is asked immediately before the experiment. A warm-up question or two between the moderator and the stimulus is cheap insurance.

Do respondents guess the hypothesis and answer accordingly? Such demand effects are a classic worry in survey experiments. Mummolo and Peterson (2019) informed respondents about the researchers’ expectations — and even paid them to respond in line with these — without consistently changing the results. This is no license to announce your hypothesis on the welcome page, but a between-subjects design, in which nobody sees the other version, is quite robust.

Mediator before or after the outcome? In a single questionnaire, both are measured within a minute, and no order can prove that the warm glow causes the willingness to pay rather than the other way round — this is the assumption behind mediation analysis that Mediation: why does it work? discusses. This course asks the mediator first, because this follows the causal order, and keeps it short. You will find both orders in the published literature; look at how your starter paper did it, decide in advance, and use the same order for every respondent.

Length

Thirty questions and less than ten minutes on a phone are the limit for a survey that people answer as a favor. Most respondents will use a phone: build short pages (one topic per page), avoid large tables, and test every page on a small screen. Every question that is not in your blueprint and does not describe your sample is a candidate for deletion.

How the randomizer works

The survey flow (Survey tab → Survey flow) is the list of elements that Qualtrics runs through from top to bottom for every respondent. Initially it contains your blocks, in the order in which you created them. A randomizer is a container in this list: for each respondent, it draws a number of the elements placed directly inside it, runs them, and skips the others. If it contains two elements and draws one, it is a coin flip. Two consequences follow, and they explain almost every mistake of the next section:

  1. Each element inside the randomizer must be one complete condition. Everything that belongs to a version — its label in the data and its stimulus — must be bundled into one element. The bundle is a Group.
  2. Everything that all respondents should see must be outside the randomizer — before it (welcome, warm-up, moderator) or after it (mediator, outcome, manipulation check, demographics).

Figure 12 shows the survey flow of the coffee study; the numbers refer to the five steps of the recipe.

ImportantThe recipe: a randomizer in five steps

Before you start: two stimulus blocks exist, and each contains only the stimulus. Open the survey flow.

  1. Add the randomizer. Click Add Below under the last block that comes before the stimulus and choose Randomizer.
  2. Add one group per condition. Click Add a New Element Here under the randomizer, choose Group, and name it (Control). Repeat for the second condition (Seal).
  3. Label the condition. In each group, add an Embedded Data element: click Create New Field or Choose From Dropdown, type the field name condition, click Set A Value Now, and type the value — Control in the first group, Seal in the second. Same field name, different values.
  4. Move the stimulus blocks in. Drag each stimulus block (click and hold Move) into its group, below the embedded data. Each stimulus block must appear in the flow exactly once — inside its group.
  5. Draw one, evenly. In the randomizer, set the number to 1 (“Randomly present 1 of the following elements”) and tick Evenly Present Elements. Click Apply.

Then compare your flow with Figure 12: the randomizer has exactly two elements directly inside it (the two groups), and every other block is outside.

Two details of the recipe deserve a comment.

The embedded data field is your treatment variable. Without it, the export does not tell you who saw which version. With it, the data contain a column condition with the values Control and Seal — the X of all your regressions. Type the field name and the values carefully (they are case-sensitive), and do not use a name that Qualtrics reserves for its own columns, such as Status.

“Evenly Present Elements” keeps the groups balanced. Without it, every respondent is a separate coin flip, and with 120 respondents a split of 54 to 66 or worse happens in about 32% of all cases. With it, Qualtrics counts how often each element was shown and keeps the counts equal. Your final groups will still not be exactly equal: the randomizer counts respondents when they pass it, not when they finish, and some respondents leave afterwards or are excluded.

Four ways to get it wrong

Figure 14 shows the four classic mistakes in the survey flow; Table 17 adds four more that do not show in the flow, and tells you how to recognize each of them.

Mistake G deserves a sentence. If each group member sends “their” version to their friends, or if respondents can choose between two links, the assignment is no longer random: who sees which version now depends on who knows whom — a confounder, built in by hand (Variables and their roles).

NoteUnder the hood: the same result with branches

There is a second correct set-up, which you may find in older materials: the randomizer contains only the two embedded data elements (condition = Control, condition = Seal), and two Branch elements below it show the stimulus (“If condition is equal to Seal” → block Stimulus – Seal; the same for Control). Step 6 · Field: pretest, launch, collect

Pretest with people, not only with test data

Test responses check the mechanics; only people can tell you whether the questionnaire is understandable. Before the launch, watch three to five people who are not in your group fill it in on a phone, and ask them to think aloud. Note where they hesitate, ask, or scroll back; how long they need; and — at the end — what they think the study was about. Revise, and test the revised version again. Delete the pretest responses before the launch: they are not part of your data.

One link for everyone

Publish the survey and distribute one anonymous link (Distributions → Anonymous link, Figure 16) — the same link for all respondents, with a neutral invitation that mentions the topic and the duration, not the hypothesis. The randomizer does the rest.

Who answers — and what that means

Your sample size calculation (Step 1) says how many respondents you need in the analysis; the guidelines ask for about 120 — about 20 per group member. Aim for about 140 started questionnaires, because some respondents will not finish and a few will be excluded. Family, friends, and fellow students are a convenience sample, and it is worth being precise about what this does and does not threaten:

  • It does not threaten the causal comparison. Whoever answers is assigned to a version by the randomizer, so the two groups are comparable — however unusual your respondents are (Randomization).
  • It does limit what the result says about the market. The effect you estimate is the effect among people like your respondents (Populations and samples). If all of them are 23-year-old business students, you know little about the customers your manager cares about — and you cannot study heterogeneity, because your moderator hardly varies. Recruit broadly, across age groups and backgrounds, and describe in your report who answered.

Decide in advance when to stop

Fix the end of the data collection before it starts — a date or a number of responses — and do not look at the difference between the conditions while the survey is running. Stopping “as soon as the result is significant” produces significant results from pure noise (Using p-values responsibly). For the same reason, do not change the stimulus, the questions, or their order after the launch. And the obvious: respondents are real people who answer once; you do not fill in your own survey, and no response is generated by a script or an AI.

NoteUnder the hood: when alpha is too low

With an alpha far below 0.7, look at the correlations between the items first. Dropping an item of a published scale needs a reason that you can state; with three items you are then left with two, and you should say so in your report. For a formative index none of this applies: its items need not be correlated.

H3: Why? The two mediation regressions, and mediate() from the mediation package (Imai et al. 2010) for the indirect effect with its bootstrap interval (Mediation: why does it work?, Figure 21). mediate() takes the two models, the name of the treatment — as a 0/1 variable, here seal from H2 — and the name of the mediator. With boot = TRUE it does what Mediation: why does it work? did by hand: it draws 1,000 bootstrap samples of the respondents, estimates both models in each of them, and reports percentile intervals. In the output, ACME (average causal mediation effect) is the indirect effect a × b, ADE (average direct effect) is the direct effect c′, and Prop. Mediated is the indirect effect as a share of the total effect.

Of the total effect of 1.28 euros (95% CI [0.63; 1.91]), 1.03 euros ran through the anticipated warm glow (indirect effect, bootstrap 95% CI [0.62; 1.50]) — about 80% of the effect. The remaining direct effect of 0.25 euros (95% CI [−0.36; 0.85]) cannot be distinguished from zero. The split into a direct and an indirect effect assumes that there are no unobserved confounders of warm glow and willingness to pay.

Read the share with care: it is the ratio of two estimates, and its interval runs from 51% to more than 100% (a share above 100% would mean that the direct effect is negative). With 120 respondents, “most of the effect” is what the data support — not a precise percentage.

The result supports the mechanism that the study measured. It does not rule out the mechanisms that were not measured (Step 1): respondents who expect a certified coffee to taste better, or who trust the brand more, may report a warmer glow as well.

From the results to the presentation

The project ends with a presentation of ten minutes for the decision maker who commissioned the study. 🎤 The example presentation of the coffee study (PDF) shows what becomes of the results of this page: ten slides after the title, one message per slide, the numbers that a critical reader needs next to every chart, a recommendation that follows from the estimates — and an appendix with the exclusions, the item statistics, the regression tables, and the robustness checks. The slides follow the nine points of the presentation guidelines; the last slide shows how the ten minutes are divided.

Use it as a model for the structure and the level of detail, not as a template: your study needs its own story.
