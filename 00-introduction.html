Introduction

Course structure

This course combines weekly in-person sessions with self-study elements on this website. The syllabus consists of three main parts:

  1. Weekly sessions & lecture notes: the in-person sessions introduce the concepts and methods — with live analyses, discussion, and plenty of time for questions. The chapters on this website consolidate each session with full explanations, commented R code, and a learning check. Work through the assigned chapter after the respective session. Each session opens with a short paper quiz on the previous chapter (counts toward participation).
  2. Individual assignments: three assignments require you to transfer the acquired knowledge to new data and business settings.
  3. Group project: you will design and conduct your own market research project — including a real randomized experiment — and present the results in a pre-recorded video presentation.

Before the second session, complete the self-paced R onboarding module, which walks you through installing R and RStudio (or working in Posit Cloud as a zero-install alternative). AI assistants are an endorsed study aid for setup problems.

If questions come up, please use the forum on Canvas and your AI assistant first — if you have the question, so do five others. Beyond that, we are available in the weekly sessions and the coaching sessions.

ImportantOur AI policy

AI assistants are welcome in the assignments — they may support your work at every step, but not do it for you: a submission without individual contribution earns no credit, disclosed or not. You must be able to explain everything you submit (that is what the debrief checks), and each report ends with a short AI-use disclosure. Honest disclosure is never penalized; concealing substantial AI use is. The final exam is the unassisted layer: paper & pencil, in person.

“How much AI is allowed?” — As much as you like, provided you can explain what was done and why.

Use it — but don’t let it study for you. Why is the policy designed this way? Recent evidence shows that the outcomes of AI use depend on how students use it. In a 30-month panel of 26,811 Chinese students, AI adoption increased homework scores by 18% and reduced homework time by 30% — but lowered exam scores by 20%, and entrance exam scores by 18–24% after two years. These losses were driven by the roughly 80% of students who outsourced their homework; students who used AI but spent the same amount of time on their homework showed no learning loss (Strömberg, Lei & Wu, 2026; see also Autor et al., 2026, NBER Working Paper 35720; Bastani et al., 2025, PNAS; Kestin et al., 2025). The course design mirrors this evidence: the assignments are your homework, with AI allowed; the debriefs and the exam are the layer where you show what you have learned.

Group project

The group project consists of two parts:

  1. Questionnaire design & data collection: your group picks one of 14 proposed topics (with starter literature) or suggests its own, and designs a questionnaire of ≈ 30 questions (~5 per member) in Qualtrics (free account provided). The design contains a real experiment with random assignment of participants to conditions. You present your draft in Coaching I (research problem, design, measurement, planned analyses), receive feedback, and revise. Each member then recruits ≈ 20 respondents, i.e. ≈ 120 responses per group.
  2. Data analysis & presentation: you import and clean your Qualtrics data in R and analyze it — descriptives, your experimental comparison, and heterogeneity (for whom does it work?). Coaching II (online) is dedicated to working through your analysis. You submit a .zip containing the pre-recorded video presentation, the data, the reproducible R code, and your slides. Afterwards, each student peer reviews two other groups’ videos (counts toward participation).

What makes a good project?

  • A precise research question with managerial relevance — and hypotheses stated before data collection
  • A clean experimental manipulation: everything constant except the treatment; randomizer placed correctly
  • Keep it simple: two conditions are sufficient (your sample size will be limited)
  • Deliberate measurement: a dependent variable with real variation (e.g., willingness to pay)
  • Moderators to analyze heterogeneous treatment responses, and mediators to understand the process
  • Analyses matched to the design — justified test choice, honest uncertainty, assumptions checked
  • A presentation that tells the decision-maker what to do — not a parade of charts

Individual grades: a peer assessment among group members and a short contribution & AI-use statement enter the individual grade.

Detailed guidelines, the topic list, and Qualtrics how-tos: see the appendices on questionnaire design and the group project.

Course materials

Main reference

The main reference for this course is this website along with the corresponding slides and PDF handouts, which are linked from every chapter. The chapters condense the contents with full explanations, commented R code, and learning checks, so that we can focus the classroom sessions on application, discussion, and the points that require clarification. Everything is free and always current — no textbook purchase is required. Canvas is used for assignment hand-outs and submissions, the forum, and announcements.

At the end of each chapter, you will find a section with references. It is highly recommended that you consult these references for further clarification in case you require additional information on a topic.

Further readings

In addition to these lecture notes, there are many excellent books available (many of them for free) that focus on different aspects of R and data analysis. There are so many free resources available by now that a team of R programmers has set up a website that provides an overview of the available resources by topic: Big Book of R.

In case you would like to learn more about the topics of this course, I can particularly recommend the following books:

  • Data Analysis for Business, Economics, and Policy (Békés & Kézdi) — an excellent, case-based treatment of data analysis, prediction, and causal inference whose spirit this course shares.
  • R for Data Science — Hadley Wickham’s introduction to R as a tool for doing data science, focusing on the tidyverse. [FREE online version]
  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning — an introduction to statistical learning methods, from linear regression to more advanced methods. [FREE online version]
  • R for Marketing Research and Analytics — designed to teach R to marketing practitioners and data scientists.
  • Statistical Inference via Data Science — an accessible treatment of statistical inference. [FREE online version]
  • Text Mining with R — analyzing unstructured data (texts) using R. [FREE online version]
  • R Graphics Cookbook — more than 150 recipes for generating high-quality graphs quickly. [FREE online version]
  • Using R For Introductory Econometrics — an introduction to R with a focus on standard tools and methods used in econometrics. [FREE online version]
  • Discovering Statistics Using R (Field, Miles & Field, 2012) — an accessible and comprehensive introduction to statistics.
